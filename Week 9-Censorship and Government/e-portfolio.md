# E-Portfolio 4: Censorship and Government

This e-portfolio demonstrates what I learned in Week 9 about censorship, internet filtering, freedom of expression and the responsibilities of governments, technology companies and ICT professionals. The artefacts show how censorship can be implemented technically, how it can affect ordinary users and how both governments and private companies can influence access to information.

## Artefact 1: China's Great Firewall and the Export of Internet Censorship

<img width="1074" height="428" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/a3c700af-f179-4a8a-a3d5-43a28cfd696a" />


**Source:** ProQuest One Academic – *Largest Ever Great Firewall Leak Reveals China's Exporting Internet Censorship Abroad*

### Summary of the Artefact

The ProQuest article reports that more than 500 GB of internal material linked to China’s Great Firewall was leaked in September 2025. The documents reportedly exposed source code, operational details and censorship technologies deployed beyond China, including Pakistan, Ethiopia, Kazakhstan and Myanmar. Examples included SIM-based tracking, regional monitoring and TLS interception (Anonymous 2025, p. 1).

### Justification and Personal Reflection

I chose this artefact because it showed me that censorship is not only about blocking websites. It can also involve surveillance and technical control of networks. I found the export of these systems concerning because technology built in one country can affect privacy and expression elsewhere. As a future ICT professional, this made me think more carefully about how systems I help create could later be used against ordinary users.

## Artefact 2: Measuring Internet Censorship Through DNS and IP Blocking

<img width="1072" height="481" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/355aecc1-964b-4f12-867d-adce62606bb8" />


**Source:** Al-Dujaily and Al-Musawi – *Measuring network-level internet censorship: DNS and IP-based filtering across Iraqi residential ISPs*

### Summary of the Artefact

Al-Dujaily and Al-Musawi measured internet censorship across Iraqi residential networks during 2025. They found DNS interference to be the most consistent blocking method, while IP blocking varied between providers. Broad filtering also caused over-blocking that affected business, IT and education services, raising concerns about transparency and proportionality (Al-Dujaily & Al-Musawi 2026, pp. 1–2).

### Justification and Personal Reflection

I chose this study because it connects directly with the technical side of our Week 9 workshop. I was surprised that a policy aimed at blocking specific content could also affect useful websites. This changed my view because censorship is not only a political decision; technical choices can create extra harm. As an IT student, I think blocking systems should be targeted, transparent and tested carefully.

## Artefact 3: Social Media, Political Speech and Private Censorship

<img width="1061" height="458" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/1113c32a-b567-435a-880e-928763bb94ba" />


**Source:** Khaled A. Beydoun – *Playing Politics: Social Media, Censorship, and Speech Gamification*

### Summary of the Artefact

Beydoun examines how social-media platforms can influence political speech even when censorship is not carried out by government. Focusing on Meta, the article argues that moderation systems can reduce the visibility of dissenting content, reward conforming speech and encourage self-censorship. It also discusses Meta’s 2025 shift toward a “more speech” approach (Beydoun 2025, p. 59).

### Justification and Personal Reflection

I chose this artefact because it challenged my assumption that censorship mainly comes from governments. Private platforms also decide what millions of people can easily see or share. I found self-censorship especially interesting because users may change what they say without being directly banned. In my view, platforms need moderation for harmful content, but their rules should be transparent and consistent.

### Summary of the Artefact

During the Week 9 workshop, the ASIC Section 313 case showed how an attempt to block fraudulent websites accidentally affected about 250,000 legitimate sites. ASIC requested an IP-address block even though the address was shared by many domains. We discussed the technical mistake, lack of transparency and whether governments and ISPs should block online content at all.

### Justification and Personal Reflection

This example stood out because it showed that censorship can fail through poor technical implementation, not only poor policy. Before the class, I mainly considered whether the government had a good reason to block harmful content. I now think accuracy and proportionality are just as important. In my view, blocking may sometimes be justified, but innocent users should not lose access because of a badly designed solution.
