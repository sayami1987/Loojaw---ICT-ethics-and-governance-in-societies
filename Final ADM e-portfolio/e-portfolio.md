Automated decision-making (ADM) uses technology to make or support decisions. In government, it can improve speed and consistency, but it can also affect people's rights, access to services and trust in public institutions. These six artefacts show how my understanding changed across the unit, especially around transparency, human oversight, fairness and professional responsibility.

## Artefact 1: Transparency of ADM in Australian government

<img width="1195" height="913" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/d68dce04-0ba2-41f7-86c1-d44020eefceb" />


**Source:** [Office of the Australian Information Commissioner – Automated decision-making and public reporting under the Freedom of Information Act (2026)](https://www.oaic.gov.au/freedom-of-information/information-commissioner-decisions-and-reports/foi-reports/Automated-decision-making-and-public-reporting-under-the-Freedom-of-Information-Act)

### Summary of the Artefact

The OAIC reviewed 23 Australian Government agencies authorised to use automated decision-making. It found that only 17% clearly disclosed their use of ADM in Information Publication Scheme material. The report recommends that agencies explain where ADM is used, what decisions are affected and what policies or rules guide the process (OAIC 2026).

### Justification and Personal Reflection

I chose this because transparency is easy to talk about but harder to see in practice. The result surprised me because I expected government agencies to clearly tell people when automation affects decisions. Social Contract Theory helped me see that government power should come with accountability to the public. As an ICT professional, I would not treat documentation as an extra task. If a system affects people's rights or services, explaining how it works should be part of responsible design.

## Artefact 2: Australia's responsible AI policy for government

<img width="1364" height="935" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/e256bd04-b1f0-47bf-8d85-41884b0064e3" />


**Source:** [Digital Transformation Agency – Policy for the responsible use of AI in government, version 2.0 (2025)](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy)

### Summary of the Artefact

Version 2.0 of Australia's government AI policy became effective on 15 December 2025. It requires non-corporate Commonwealth entities to use accountable officials, transparency statements, internal AI use-case registers, staff training and impact assessments. The policy recognises AI's benefits but says government has an elevated responsibility to use it safely and ethically (Digital Transformation Agency 2025).

### Justification and Personal Reflection

This changed my view that ethical technology is mainly about whether a model is accurate. I now see that responsibility also depends on governance around the system: who is accountable, whether risks are assessed and whether staff understand the technology. Rule Utilitarianism fits this well because consistent safety rules can protect the public across many systems. I think developers should understand these controls rather than leaving ethics only to managers.

## Artefact 3: Robodebt and the need for review rights

<img width="1777" height="906" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/f81f3b85-a1f8-480c-8a66-f37cc4fe4e58" />


**Source:** [Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet – Robodebt Royal Commission Implementation Update, March 2026](https://www.pmc.gov.au/sites/default/files/resource/download/robodebt-royal-commission-implementation-update-march-2026.pdf)

### Summary of the Artefact

The March 2026 Robodebt implementation update lists Recommendation 17.1 as ongoing. It calls for a consistent legal framework for government automation, clear review pathways, plain-language explanations of ADM, and access to business rules or algorithms for independent scrutiny (Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet 2026).

### Justification and Personal Reflection

I selected this because Robodebt shows that ADM can directly affect people who depend on public services. Kantianism matters here because people should be treated with dignity, not simply as records processed for efficiency. The importance of being able to challenge a decision stood out to me. If I worked on an ADM system, I would treat review and appeal pathways as basic requirements, not fixes added after problems occur.

## Artefact 4: Human rights protections for government ADM

<img width="1834" height="892" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/441bc65b-7601-443e-8e60-9c5bccf41f4c" />


**Source:** [Australian Human Rights Commission – Use of automated decision making by government (2025)](https://humanrights.gov.au/resource-hub/by-resource-type/submissions/use-of-automated-decision-making-by-government)

### Summary of the Artefact

The Australian Human Rights Commission argues that government ADM should be built around human rights protections. Its 2025 submission recommends human rights impact assessments, notice when ADM materially affects a decision, understandable reasons and independent oversight. It links these safeguards to fairness, access to justice and public trust (Australian Human Rights Commission 2025).

### Justification and Personal Reflection

I chose this because it focuses on the person affected rather than only the agency using the system. The ACS Code also places public interest and quality of life above convenience. I agree that human review must be meaningful. A reviewer should be able to question an automated result and correct it when needed, not simply approve what the system produced. This made me think more about how design choices can protect or weaken people's rights.

## Artefact 5: What research says about ADM in public administration

<img width="1053" height="772" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/6c63ee2d-4814-4588-a9f3-f7ced36de8da" />

**Source:** [Rizk and Lindgren – Automated decision-making in public administration: Changing the decision space between public officials and citizens (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.giq.2025.102061)

### Summary of the Artefact

Rizk and Lindgren explain that ADM in public administration creates tensions between efficiency and fair decisions, transparency and privacy, and standardisation and human discretion (Rizk & Lindgren 2025, p. 1). Their research shows that ADM changes the relationship between citizens, public officials and government organisations rather than simply replacing one manual task with software.

### Justification and Personal Reflection

I included this article because it gave me a broader view than the policy documents. Act Utilitarianism can support automation when it improves services for many people, but that benefit cannot be judged only by speed. Harm to fairness, privacy or trust also matters. I learned that ICT professionals need to think about the whole decision process, including what humans still control and how citizens experience the outcome.

## Artefact 6: Canada's Claim Summary Tool for veterans

<img width="1512" height="908" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/095395ed-7e91-4444-9c33-3e1e0978fd7d" />


**Source:** [Veterans Affairs Canada – Claim Summary Tool (2026)](https://www.veterans.gc.ca/en/mental-and-physical-health/physical-health-and-wellness/compensation-illness-or-injury/disability-benefits/claim-summary-tool)

**Supporting assessment:** [Government of Canada – Algorithmic Impact Assessment: Claim Summary Tool (2026)](https://open.canada.ca/data/en/info/c839bedc-0d5f-4b02-98a7-671d1f60ac51)

### Summary of the Artefact

Veterans Affairs Canada uses the AI-enabled Claim Summary Tool to organise information in Disability Benefit applications and highlight key details for staff. The tool does not decide eligibility or make the final decision; trained employees remain responsible. Canada also published an Algorithmic Impact Assessment covering privacy, oversight and ongoing monitoring (Veterans Affairs Canada 2026).

### Justification and Personal Reflection

I chose this as an international example because it shows ADM in a real government service while keeping a human decision maker involved. It also gives the public information about the system and an impact assessment. Compared with the transparency problems found in Australia, this seems more open. However, human involvement alone does not guarantee fairness because staff can rely too heavily on automated summaries. My main lesson is that human oversight, transparency and regular testing need to work together.
