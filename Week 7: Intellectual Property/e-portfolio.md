## Artefact 1: Copyright and Artificial Intelligence in Australia

<img width="664" height="704" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/ec4e8567-bd49-413d-a263-aa17e88f564a" />


**Source:** [Australian Government – Copyright and AI Transparency Discussion Paper](https://www.ag.gov.au/rights-and-protections/publications/copyright-and-ai-transparency-discussion-paper)

### Summary of the Artefact

The Australian Attorney-General’s Department published a Copyright and AI transparency discussion paper in 2025 as part of its ongoing work with the Copyright and Artificial Intelligence Reference Group. The government is considering how copyright law should respond when protected works are used in AI systems and whether greater transparency is needed. Important questions include identifying copyright material used in AI training and giving creators enough information to understand how their work has been used.

### Justification and Personal Reflection

I chose this artefact because it shows that AI copyright is not only an overseas issue; Australia is actively considering how its own copyright system should respond. As an IT student studying in Australia, I found this especially relevant. Before this workshop, I mainly thought copyright was about directly copying another person’s work. I now realise that AI creates a less visible problem because copyrighted material can be used during model training without the creator necessarily knowing. I believe transparency is important because creators cannot exercise their rights properly if they do not know how their work has been used.

## Artefact 2: Music Companies Challenge AI Training

![News article about Sony and Warner Music suing Anthropic](images/artefact-2-anthropic-copyright.png)

**Source:** [Reuters – Sony and Warner Music sue Anthropic over AI training](https://www.reuters.com/legal/government/sony-warner-music-sue-anthropic-over-songs-used-ai-training-2026-08-31/)

### Summary of the Artefact

A recent copyright dispute involves Sony Music and Warner Music suing AI company Anthropic. The music companies allege that copyrighted song lyrics and sheet music were unlawfully used to train Anthropic’s Claude models and argue that AI-generated material can compete with legitimate copyrighted works. Anthropic has defended AI training as fair use. The case demonstrates the growing conflict between technology companies that require large datasets and creators who want control and compensation for their intellectual property (Reuters, 2026).

### Justification and Personal Reflection

I selected this case because it made the copyright issue feel current rather than theoretical. I use generative AI regularly, but I normally only see the final output and do not think about all the material that may have contributed to training the model. I felt concerned when considering that musicians and writers could lose control over how their creative work is used. At the same time, restricting all training data could slow useful innovation. This artefact helped me understand the compromise discussed in our workshop: intellectual property protection should reward creativity, but society also benefits when knowledge and technology can develop.

## Artefact 3: Copyrighted Works Used for Generative AI Training

![Scholarly article about copyright and AI training](images/artefact-3-ai-training-copyright.png)

**Source:** [Control and Compensation: A Comparative Analysis of Copyright Exceptions for Training Generative AI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40319-025-01569-6)

### Summary of the Artefact

De la Durantaye examines how different countries are responding to the use of copyrighted material for training generative AI. The article identifies four major issues: **control, compensation, transparency and legal certainty**. Some legal systems give copyright owners greater control over whether their works can be used, while others provide exceptions for text and data mining. Transparency is particularly important because creators may find it difficult to enforce their rights when AI training occurs without them seeing exactly which works were used (de la Durantaye, 2025, pp. 737–739).

### Justification and Personal Reflection

I chose this scholarly article because it helped me see that there is no simple international solution to AI copyright. I originally assumed copyright rules would apply similarly everywhere, but countries balance innovation and creator rights differently. The ideas of control and compensation stood out to me. If an AI company benefits commercially from thousands of creative works, I think it is reasonable to ask whether creators should have some control or receive compensation. As a future ICT professional, this reminds me that legal access to data is not the only consideration; I should also think about fairness, transparency and the people who created the information.

## Artefact 4: Week 7 Workshop – Authorship, Copyright and the Monkey Selfie

| **Tutor** | Umapathy Venugopal |
| **Campus** | Sydney |
| **Workshop topic** | Intellectual Property |


### Summary of the Artefact

A key idea from the Week 7 workshop was the **Monkey Selfie copyright dispute**. Photographer David Slater prepared the camera and environment, but a macaque pressed the shutter and created the photographs. The dispute raised the question of who could be considered the author. The workshop connected this case to AI because AI systems also produce content without being human creators. Under current approaches discussed in class, human authorship remains important and AI itself cannot own copyright.

### Justification and Personal Reflection

I chose this workshop example because it made the idea of authorship much easier for me to understand. Before the discussion, I would probably have assumed that whoever owned the camera or AI software automatically owned the final work. The monkey-selfie example showed me that copyright is more complicated because authorship depends on human creative contribution. I found the comparison with generative AI particularly interesting. It made me question how much human involvement is enough when someone only enters a short prompt. My main lesson is that technology is changing much faster than traditional ideas about authorship and ownership.
